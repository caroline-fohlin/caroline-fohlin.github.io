# “Rethinking the Lender of Last Resort: New Evidence on the Stabilization of Money Markets Before the Federal Reserve” 
Presentations: Sveriges Riksbank (November 2024), Federal Reserve Workshop on Monetary and Financial History, CEBRA Annual Conference (2021 online), CEPR Economic History Workshop Barcelona (2019), World Cliometric Conference (2017).

Abstract:
Short-term funding markets play a critical role in financial stability. Liquidity shocks regularly disrupted money markets historically, causing interest rate volatility and strong seasonality; a problem commonly thought solved by the creation of the Federal Reserve System in 1913-4. In contrast, this paper demonstrates that interest rates
stabilized six years earlier, following large-scale imports of gold from France to quell the Panic of 1907 and the subsequent creation of a national lender of last resort mechanism under the Aldrich-Vreeland Act in 1908. The founding of the Fed generated little additional impact on funding market rates or short-term credit spreads. Moreover, the Fed failed to attract banks into its nascent discount market, while they could earn higher returns funding the rapidly expanding stock market between World War I and the 1929 crash.

# “Hedging Against Turmoil: Asset Pricing, Liquidity, and Equity Market Dynamics in the German Hyperinflation,” (With Stephanie Collet) Previously entitled “The Berlin Stock Exchange in the Great Disorder.” 
Presentations: Paris School of Economics (May 2025), Yale Economics Department seminar (October 2023), CEPR Economic History Workshop, University of Geneva (June 2023), London School of Economics (June 2022), Federal Reserve Bank Workshop on Monetary and Financial History (May 2022), CEPR Interna-
tional Macrohistory Workshop Online (November 2021), International Economic History Association
World Congress, Boston (2018).

Abstract:
Based on the first ever daily database of all stocks trading on the Berlin Stock Exchange during the interwar years, this paper analyzes the functioning of Germany's premier financial market over the crisis of the post-World War I hyperinflation, political upheaval, new currency, and subsequent stabilization. We show that, despite the severity of the hyperinflation and the extreme uncertainty surrounding political and economic conditions, the market remained remarkably active and liquid throughout peak hyperinflation of 1923 and stabilization in 1924 and returned to normal patterns of performance by 1925. Surprisingly, companies created after the start of inflation in 1921 do not seem to incur additional illiquidity during the hyperinflation, but these same stocks became less liquid after stabilization. This is also the first paper to systematically analyze information on the level of trading and direction and extent of order imbalance in the German stock market. We find that demand exceeded supply most days during the period and especially during and after the end of the hyperinflation. We additionally find that equities worked relatively well as an inflation hedge, but that the government's announcement of a proposed wealth tax interrupted that process by causing a sharp revaluation of stocks (though the market quickly recovered). Thus, we demonstrate for the first time on a day-by-day basis that Germany's financial market functioned with resilience and provided a critical store of value for investors during one of the most extreme episodes of hyperinflation and political-economic instability in history. Moreover, anticipation of monetary stabilization worked quickly, smoothly, and powerfully to bring financial markets back into line with normal valuations.
 
# “Insider Trading and Market Efficiency before the SEC: Evidence from the Teapot Dome Scandal” (with Noah MacDonald) 
Presentations: Business History Conference (2025), Economic History Association (2023), World Cliometrics Society (2023).

Abstract:
We examine insider trading during the Teapot Dome scandal to evaluate stock market efficiency prior to the establishment of the SEC and the regulation of insider trading. Our findings suggest that insiders refrained from fully exploiting their private information due to concerns over detection from other traders, competitors, or from congressional scrutiny, preventing stock prices from fully reflecting insider knowledge as predicted by the strong form of the efficient market hypothesis. Nevertheless, insiders still realized substantial gains, amounting to approximately $200 million in today’s dollars. We also document that, in the absence of formal insider trading regulation, market makers responded by widening bid-ask spreads for firms suspected of insider activity.

# “Short Sale Bans May Improve Market Quality During Crises: New Evidence from the 2020 Covid Crash,” With Zhikun Lu and Nan Zhou, CEPR Discussion Paper No. 17725. (Updated 2024). VoxEU column.

Abstract:
Short selling bans may stabilize stock prices during crises but could simultaneously harm market quality by constraining information revelation. Past studies have produced mixed findings. This paper uses cross-country policy variation during the 2020 Covid crisis to examine how bans impact stock liquidity, prices, and volatility. We
find that bans improve liquidity and reduce volatility for illiquid stocks but diminish liquidity for liquid stocks. Our findings support theories that short sale bans filter out informed traders who may harm liquidity. Therefore, policies that target only illiquid stocks may enhance market quality better than uniform bans targeting all stocks.
